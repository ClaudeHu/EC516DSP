## 1. Signals and index operations

### 1.1 Impulse and step

$$
\delta[n]=u[n]-u[n-1],\qquad u[n]=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{n}\delta[k]
$$

**Sifting representation.** Any sequence is a sum of scaled, shifted impulses. This is the fact that makes convolution work.

$$
x[n]=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}x[k] \delta[n-k]
$$

**Multiplying by an impulse is not the same as convolving with one.**

| Operation | Result | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| $x[n] \delta[n-n_0]$ | $x[n_0] \delta[n-n_0]$ | Picks out one sample |
| $x[n] \ast \delta[n-n_0]$ | $x[n-n_0]$ | Shifts the whole sequence |

Example: $x[n]$ is the track and $n_0= 480$ samples (10 ms at 48 kHz).

Convolving with $\delta[n-n_0]$ is moving the mic back. Clap once and the mic hears one clap, 480 samples later. That is what you get by moving the mic about 3.4 m farther from the guitar (ignoring the level drop), or by nudging the track 480 samples to the right in your DAW.

Multiplying by $\delta[n-n_0]$ is a gate that opens for one sample. Multiplication is point by point, and $\delta[n-n_0]$ is zero everywhere except $n=n_0$, so the gate mutes the entire track except that one instant

### 1.2 Operations on the index

| Expression | Effect |
|---|---|
| $x[n-n_0]$ | Shift right by $n_0$ (left if $n_0 < 0$) |
| $x[-n]$ | Flip about $n=0$ |
| $x[n_0-n]$ | Flip, then shift right by $n_0$ |
| $x[Mn]$ | Keep every $M$-th sample; the others are discarded |
| $x[n] u[n_0-n]$ | Keep $n\le n_0$, zero the rest |

**Rule that never fails:** the sample that sits at index $m$ in $x$ appears where the argument equals $m$. For $x[n_0-n]$, solve $n_0-n=m$ to get $n=n_0-m$. Test one sample before drawing the rest.

### 1.3 Sinusoids and complex exponentials

$$
\cos\theta=\tfrac12\left(e^{j\theta}+e^{-j\theta}\right),\qquad \sin\theta=\tfrac{1}{2j}\left(e^{j\theta}-e^{-j\theta}\right)
$$

**Frequency is only defined modulo $2\pi$.** Because $n$ is an integer,

$$
e^{j(\omega_0+2\pi k)n}=e^{j\omega_0 n}
$$

- All distinct frequencies live in $(-\pi,\pi]$. $\omega=0$ is the lowest (DC), $\omega=\pi$ is the highest, where $e^{j\pi n}=(-1)^n$.
- Reducing a frequency into $(-\pi,\pi]$ can make it negative. Then $\cos(-\theta)=\cos\theta$ flips the sign of the constant phase as well:

$$
\cos\left(\tfrac{3\pi}{2}n+\phi\right)=\cos\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}n+\phi\right)=\cos\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}n-\phi\right)
$$

> **Picture it (race cars).** This is the wagon-wheel effect in onboard footage. A wheel that turns $\tfrac{15}{8}$ of a revolution between frames ($\tfrac{15\pi}{4}$ rad) looks exactly like one turning $\tfrac18$ of a revolution backward ($-\tfrac{\pi}{4}$ rad). The camera only sees the angle modulo one turn, and a sequence only sees $\omega$ modulo $2\pi$.

**Useful half-angle factorizations**

$$
1-e^{-j\omega}=2j\sin(\omega/2) e^{-j\omega/2},\qquad 1+e^{-j\omega}=2\cos(\omega/2) e^{-j\omega/2}
$$

**Combining a cosine and a sine**

$$
A\cos\theta+B\sin\theta=\sqrt{A^2+B^2} \cos\big(\theta-\mathrm{atan2}(B,A)\big)
$$

## 2. LTI systems and convolution

### 2.1 Definitions

- **Linear:** $T\lbrace a x_1+b x_2\rbrace=a T\lbrace x_1\rbrace+b T\lbrace x_2\rbrace$
- **Time-invariant:** if $x[n]\to y[n]$ then $x[n-n_0]\to y[n-n_0]$
- **Causal:** $y[n]$ depends only on $x[k]$ for $k\le n$. For LTI: $h[n]=0$ for $n < 0$
- **BIBO stable:** bounded input gives bounded output. For LTI: $\sum_n\lvert h[n]\rvert < \infty$

### 2.2 Why convolution

Write $x$ as a sum of impulses (sifting). Linearity lets you treat each impulse separately; time invariance says the response to $\delta[n-k]$ is $h[n-k]$. So an LTI system is completely described by its impulse response $h[n]$:

$$
y[n]=x[n] \ast h[n]=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}x[k] h[n-k]
$$

Two ways to read the sum, both worth having:

- **Stack copies:** every input sample $x[k]$ launches a copy of $h$, scaled by $x[k]$ and shifted to start at $k$. Add the copies. Best when $x$ is a few impulses.
- **Flip and slide:** flip $h$, slide it to position $n$, multiply by $x$ and sum. Best when both are long or defined by formulas.

> **Picture it (guitar recording).** Clap once in a recital hall and record it: that is the hall's impulse response. A dry close-mic guitar track convolved with it is convolution reverb. Each sample of the dry track launches its own scaled, delayed copy of the hall's response, and what you hear is the sum of all the copies.

### 2.3 Properties of convolution

| Property | Statement | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Commutative | $x \ast h=h \ast x$ | Either signal can be the one you flip |
| Associative | $(x \ast h_1) \ast h_2=x \ast (h_1 \ast h_2)$ | Cascade: $h=h_1 \ast h_2$, order does not matter |
| Distributive | $x \ast (h_1+h_2)=x \ast h_1+x \ast h_2$ | Parallel: $h=h_1+h_2$ |
| Identity | $x \ast \delta=x$ | |
| Shift | $x[n] \ast \delta[n-n_0]=x[n-n_0]$ | Delays add: if $x \ast h=y$ then $x[n-a] \ast h[n-b]=y[n-a-b]$ |
| Running sum | $x[n] \ast u[n]=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{n}x[k]$ | Step response $s[n]=\sum_{k\le n}h[k]$, and $h[n]=s[n]-s[n-1]$ |

### 2.4 Facts about finite-length convolution

- **Support adds:** if $x$ lives on $[N_1,N_2]$ and $h$ on $[M_1,M_2]$, then $y$ lives on $[N_1+M_1, N_2+M_2]$. Length $=L_x+L_h-1$.
- **Sum multiplies:** $\sum_n y[n]=\left(\sum_n x[n]\right)\left(\sum_n h[n]\right)$
- **Rectangle with rectangle:** two length-$N$ rectangles give a triangle of length $2N-1$ with peak $N$. Unequal lengths give a trapezoid.
- **Step into an FIR system:** the output climbs through the running sum of $h$ and then stays at $\sum h[n]$. If $\sum h[n]=0$ it returns to zero.

### 2.5 Using linearity and time invariance instead of computing

If you already know the response $y_a[n]$ to some input $x_a[n]$:

$$
x[n]=\sum_i c_i x_a[n-n_i]\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y[n]=\sum_i c_i y_a[n-n_i]
$$

A rectangle is a difference of steps, $u[n]-u[n-N]$, so its response is $s[n]-s[n-N]$. Look for this before starting a convolution from scratch.

## 3. Frequency response

### 3.1 Eigenfunction property

Put $x[n]=e^{j\omega_0 n}$ (for **all** $n$) into the convolution sum:

$$
y[n]=\sum_k h[k] e^{j\omega_0(n-k)}=\underbrace{\left(\sum_k h[k]e^{-j\omega_0 k}\right)}_{H(e^{j\omega_0})}e^{j\omega_0 n}
$$

A complex exponential comes out as the same exponential times a complex constant. The system cannot create new frequencies.

$$
H(e^{j\omega})=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}h[k] e^{-j\omega k}=\lvert H(e^{j\omega})\rvert e^{j\angle H(e^{j\omega})}
$$

- $\lvert H\rvert$ is the gain at that frequency; $\angle H$ is the phase shift.
- $H(e^{j\omega})$ is periodic with period $2\pi$. It is specified on $(-\pi,\pi]$, so **reduce the input frequency into that interval before reading it off**.
- It exists (as an ordinary function) when the system is stable.

> **Picture it (guitar recording).** Play a clean 12th-fret harmonic through an EQ. It comes out at the same pitch, only louder or quieter and slightly shifted in time. The EQ curve is $\lvert H\rvert$; no setting of an EQ can turn that harmonic into a different note.

### 3.2 Sinusoid in, sinusoid out

**If $h[n]$ is real**, then $H(e^{-j\omega})=H^{\ast}(e^{j\omega})$: magnitude is even and phase is odd. The two exponentials inside a cosine get conjugate gains and recombine into a real cosine:

$$
A\cos(\omega_0 n+\phi) \longrightarrow A \lvert H(e^{j\omega_0})\rvert \cos\big(\omega_0 n+\phi+\angle H(e^{j\omega_0})\big)
$$

**If $H$ is not conjugate-symmetric** ($h[n]$ complex), that shortcut is invalid. Split the cosine with Euler, multiply each exponential by its own $H(e^{\pm j\omega_0})$, and add. The output is generally complex.

Quick test: is the magnitude even and the phase odd? A phase like $-\tfrac{\omega}{2}-\tfrac{\pi}{4}$ fails because of the constant term.

### 3.3 Linear phase is delay

$$
H(e^{j\omega})=e^{-j\omega n_d}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad y[n]=x[n-n_d]
$$

For a sinusoid this works even when $n_d$ is not an integer: replace $n$ with $n-n_d$. A constant factor $e^{j\theta_0}$ just multiplies the output by a complex constant. So $H=e^{j\theta_0}e^{-j\omega n_d}$ means "delay by $n_d$, then multiply by $e^{j\theta_0}$".

### 3.4 Input for all $n$ versus input switched on at $n=0$

- $e^{j\omega_0 n}$ for all $n$: the output is exactly $H(e^{j\omega_0})e^{j\omega_0 n}$.
- $e^{j\omega_0 n}u[n]$ into a stable causal system: output = steady-state $H(e^{j\omega_0})e^{j\omega_0 n}$ **plus a transient** that dies out.
- If $H(e^{j\omega_0})=0$, only the transient remains. A length-4 moving sum has a null at $\omega=\pi$, so $(-1)^n u[n]$ produces a few start-up samples and then zero.

### 3.5 Ideal filters

| Filter | $H(e^{j\omega})$ on $\lvert\omega\rvert\le\pi$ | $h[n]$ |
|---|---|---|
| Lowpass, cutoff $\omega_c$ | 1 for $\lvert\omega\rvert\le\omega_c$, else 0 | $\dfrac{\sin(\omega_c n)}{\pi n}$, with $h[0]=\dfrac{\omega_c}{\pi}$ |
| Highpass | $1-H_{lp}$ | $\delta[n]-\dfrac{\sin(\omega_c n)}{\pi n}$ |
| Bandpass, $\omega_1 < \lvert\omega\rvert\le\omega_2$ | $H_{lp,\omega_2}-H_{lp,\omega_1}$ | $\dfrac{\sin(\omega_2 n)-\sin(\omega_1 n)}{\pi n}$ |
| Any of these with delay $n_d$ | multiply by $e^{-j\omega n_d}$ | replace $n$ with $n-n_d$ |

- A gain $G$ in the passband multiplies $h[n]$ by $G$. Read the gain and cutoff of a sinc-type $h[n]$ straight from its formula.
- Ideal filters are noncausal and infinitely long.
- A sinusoid inside the passband passes with the passband gain and phase; one in the stopband is removed completely.

### 3.6 Interconnections

$$
\text{Cascade:} H=H_1H_2\qquad\qquad\text{Parallel:} H=H_1+H_2
$$

For a cascade of piecewise filters, multiply band by band: the result is nonzero only where **both** are nonzero. Magnitudes multiply and phases add.

### 3.7 Two building blocks worth memorizing

**First difference** $y[n]=x[n]-x[n-1]$:

$$
H(e^{j\omega})=1-e^{-j\omega}=2\sin(\omega/2) e^{j(\pi/2-\omega/2)}\qquad(0 < \omega < \pi)
$$

Highpass, zero gain at DC, and it turns a step into an impulse: $u[n]-u[n-1]=\delta[n]$.

**Moving sum** of length $M+1$, $h[n]=1$ for $0\le n\le M$:

$$
H(e^{j\omega})=\frac{\sin\big(\omega(M+1)/2\big)}{\sin(\omega/2)} e^{-j\omega M/2}
$$

Lowpass, DC gain $M+1$, nulls at $\omega=\dfrac{2\pi k}{M+1}$ for $k\ne0$.

> **Picture it (watches).** Log a watch's accumulated error against a reference each morning. The first difference of that log is the daily rate, and a constant offset (you set it 10 s fast) disappears because the gain at DC is zero. Going the other way, accumulated error is the running sum of the daily rate, which is convolution with $u[n]$.

## 4. DTFT

### 4.1 Definition

$$
X(e^{j\omega})=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x[n] e^{-j\omega n},\qquad x[n]=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}X(e^{j\omega}) e^{j\omega n} d\omega
$$

- Always periodic in $\omega$ with period $2\pi$.
- Converges uniformly if $x[n]$ is absolutely summable. Square-summable sequences (the ideal-filter sincs) converge in the mean-square sense. Sequences such as $1$, $u[n]$ and $\cos(\omega_0 n)$ need impulses in $\omega$.
- The frequency response is the DTFT of the impulse response.

### 4.2 Symmetry for real $x[n]$

$$
X(e^{-j\omega})=X^{\ast}(e^{j\omega})
$$

| Even in $\omega$ | Odd in $\omega$ |
|---|---|
| $\mathrm{Re}\lbrace X\rbrace$, $\lvert X\rvert$ | $\mathrm{Im}\lbrace X\rbrace$, $\angle X$ |

- Real and even $x[n]$ gives real and even $X$. Real and odd $x[n]$ gives purely imaginary and odd $X$.
- Odd functions are zero at $\omega=0$. Use this to anchor sketches.
- To sketch on $[-\pi,\pi]$: compute the values at $0$ and $\pi$, apply the symmetry, then locate any interior peak.

### 4.3 Getting real part, imaginary part, magnitude and phase

For a rational expression, multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator. For the first-order case, with $D(\omega)=1-2a\cos\omega+a^2$:

$$
\frac{1}{1-ae^{-j\omega}}=\frac{(1-a\cos\omega)-j a\sin\omega}{D(\omega)}
$$

$$
\lvert X\rvert=\frac{1}{\sqrt{D(\omega)}},\qquad\angle X=-\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{a\sin\omega}{1-a\cos\omega}\right)
$$

What to understand about this pair ($a^n u[n]$, $\lvert a\rvert < 1$):

- Value at $\omega=0$ is $\tfrac{1}{1-a}$ and at $\omega=\pi$ is $\tfrac{1}{1+a}$.
- $0 < a < 1$: **lowpass**. $-1 < a < 0$: **highpass**, because $a^n=(-1)^n\lvert a\rvert^n$ and multiplying by $(-1)^n$ shifts a spectrum by $\pi$.
- Geometric view: $\lvert X\rvert=\dfrac{1}{\lvert e^{j\omega}-a\rvert}$, one over the distance from the point $e^{j\omega}$ on the unit circle to the pole at $a$. The response is largest where the unit circle passes closest to the pole.
- The phase is bounded: its peak size is $\sin^{-1}\lvert a\rvert$, reached where $\cos\omega=a$.

### 4.4 Transform pairs

| $x[n]$ | $X(e^{j\omega})$ (one period) |
|---|---|
| $\delta[n]$ | $1$ |
| $\delta[n-n_0]$ | $e^{-j\omega n_0}$ |
| $a^n u[n]$, $\lvert a\rvert < 1$ | $\dfrac{1}{1-ae^{-j\omega}}$ |
| $(n+1)a^n u[n]$, $\lvert a\rvert < 1$ | $\dfrac{1}{(1-ae^{-j\omega})^2}$ |
| $\dfrac{\sin(\omega_c n)}{\pi n}$ | $1$ for $\lvert\omega\rvert\le\omega_c$, else $0$ |
| $1$ for $0\le n\le M$, else $0$ | $\dfrac{\sin(\omega(M+1)/2)}{\sin(\omega/2)} e^{-j\omega M/2}$ |
| $1$ | $2\pi \delta(\omega)$ |
| $e^{j\omega_0 n}$ | $2\pi \delta(\omega-\omega_0)$ |
| $\cos(\omega_0 n+\phi)$ | $\pi e^{j\phi}\delta(\omega-\omega_0)+\pi e^{-j\phi}\delta(\omega+\omega_0)$ |
| $u[n]$ | $\dfrac{1}{1-e^{-j\omega}}+\pi \delta(\omega)$ |

### 4.5 Properties

| Property | Time | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Linearity | $ax[n]+by[n]$ | $aX+bY$ |
| Time shift | $x[n-n_d]$ | $e^{-j\omega n_d}X(e^{j\omega})$ |
| Frequency shift | $e^{j\omega_0 n}x[n]$ | $X(e^{j(\omega-\omega_0)})$ |
| Modulation by $(-1)^n$ | $(-1)^n x[n]$ | $X(e^{j(\omega-\pi)})$ |
| Time reversal | $x[-n]$ | $X(e^{-j\omega})$ |
| Conjugation | $x^{\ast}[n]$ | $X^{\ast}(e^{-j\omega})$ |
| Differentiation | $n x[n]$ | $j \dfrac{dX}{d\omega}$ |
| Convolution | $x[n] \ast y[n]$ | $X Y$ |
| Multiplication | $x[n] y[n]$ | $\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}X(e^{j\theta})Y(e^{j(\omega-\theta)}) d\theta$ |
| Parseval | $\displaystyle\sum_n\lvert x[n]\rvert^2$ | $\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\lvert X\rvert^2 d\omega$ |

A time shift changes only the phase (adds $-\omega n_d$); the magnitude is untouched.

### 4.6 Inverse DTFT in practice

1. **Recognize pairs first.** Decompose into lowpass pieces, pull out $e^{-j\omega n_d}$ as a delay, pull out constants as gains.
2. **Otherwise integrate.** For a real impulse response with symmetric bands, the two sides combine into a cosine integral:

   $$
   \frac{1}{2\pi}\left[\int_{-\omega_2}^{-\omega_1}+\int_{\omega_1}^{\omega_2}\right]G e^{j\omega(n-n_d)} d\omega=\frac{G}{\pi}\int_{\omega_1}^{\omega_2}\cos\big(\omega(n-n_d)\big) d\omega
   $$

3. **Handle the $0/0$ sample separately.** At $n=n_d$ the sinc formula is undefined; take the limit or just integrate the constant. State that value explicitly.

### 4.7 Special values

$$
X(e^{j0})=\sum_n x[n],\qquad X(e^{j\pi})=\sum_n(-1)^n x[n],\qquad x[0]=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}X(e^{j\omega}) d\omega
$$

## 5. z-transform

### 5.1 Definition and link to the DTFT

$$
X(z)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x[n] z^{-n},\qquad z=re^{j\omega}
$$

$X(z)$ on the circle $\lvert z\rvert=r$ is the DTFT of $x[n]r^{-n}$. So:

- The **ROC** is the set of $z$ where the sum converges, and it depends only on $\lvert z\rvert$.
- **The DTFT is the z-transform on the unit circle, and it exists only if the ROC includes $\lvert z\rvert=1$.**
- The same algebraic $X(z)$ with different ROCs is a different sequence. An answer without its ROC is incomplete.

### 5.2 ROC properties

1. The ROC is a ring or disk centered at the origin: $r_R < \lvert z\rvert < r_L$.
2. The ROC contains no poles and is bounded by poles.
3. The ROC is one connected region.
4. **Finite-length** sequence: the whole plane, except possibly $z=0$ (if there are samples at $n > 0$) and $z=\infty$ (if there are samples at $n < 0$).
5. **Right-sided** sequence: outside the outermost pole.
6. **Left-sided** sequence: inside the innermost pole.
7. **Two-sided** sequence: a ring between two poles, or it does not exist.

With $N$ distinct pole radii there are at most $N+1$ possible ROCs, each a different sequence.

### 5.3 Transform pairs

| $x[n]$ | $X(z)$ | ROC |
|---|---|---|
| $\delta[n]$ | $1$ | all $z$ |
| $\delta[n-m]$ | $z^{-m}$ | all $z$ except $0$ ($m > 0$) or $\infty$ ($m < 0$) |
| $u[n]$ | $\dfrac{1}{1-z^{-1}}$ | $\lvert z\rvert > 1$ |
| $-u[-n-1]$ | $\dfrac{1}{1-z^{-1}}$ | $\lvert z\rvert < 1$ |
| $a^n u[n]$ | $\dfrac{1}{1-az^{-1}}$ | $\lvert z\rvert > \lvert a\rvert$ |
| $-a^n u[-n-1]$ | $\dfrac{1}{1-az^{-1}}$ | $\lvert z\rvert < \lvert a\rvert$ |
| $n a^n u[n]$ | $\dfrac{az^{-1}}{(1-az^{-1})^2}$ | $\lvert z\rvert > \lvert a\rvert$ |
| $r^n\cos(\omega_0 n) u[n]$ | $\dfrac{1-r\cos\omega_0 z^{-1}}{1-2r\cos\omega_0 z^{-1}+r^2z^{-2}}$ | $\lvert z\rvert > r$ |
| $r^n\sin(\omega_0 n) u[n]$ | $\dfrac{r\sin\omega_0 z^{-1}}{1-2r\cos\omega_0 z^{-1}+r^2z^{-2}}$ | $\lvert z\rvert > r$ |
| $a^n$ for $0\le n\le N-1$ | $\dfrac{1-a^Nz^{-N}}{1-az^{-1}}$ | $\lvert z\rvert > 0$ |

The right-sided and left-sided exponentials share the same formula. Only the ROC and a minus sign distinguish them.

A complex-conjugate pole pair at $re^{\pm j\omega_0}$ gives a term $r^n\cos(\omega_0 n+\phi)$: the **radius sets the decay rate, the angle sets the oscillation frequency**.

> **Picture it (guitar).** A plucked string is a pole pair inside the unit circle: the angle is the pitch and the radius is the sustain. Move the pair toward the circle and the note rings longer. Put it outside and you get microphone feedback, growing instead of decaying.

### 5.4 Properties

| Property | Sequence | Transform | ROC |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linearity | $ax_1[n]+bx_2[n]$ | $aX_1+bX_2$ | contains $R_1\cap R_2$ |
| Time shift | $x[n-n_0]$ | $z^{-n_0}X(z)$ | $R$, except possibly $0$ or $\infty$ |
| Exponential scaling | $z_0^n x[n]$ | $X(z/z_0)$ | $\lvert z_0\rvert R$ |
| Differentiation | $n x[n]$ | $-z \dfrac{dX}{dz}$ | $R$ |
| Conjugation | $x^{\ast}[n]$ | $X^{\ast}(z^{\ast})$ | $R$ |
| Time reversal | $x[-n]$ | $X(1/z)$ | $1/R$ |
| Convolution | $x_1[n] \ast x_2[n]$ | $X_1(z)X_2(z)$ | contains $R_1\cap R_2$ |
| Initial value | $x[n]=0$ for $n < 0$ | $x[0]=\lim_{z\to\infty}X(z)$ | |

"Contains" matters: a pole-zero cancellation can make the ROC larger than the intersection.

### 5.5 Poles and zeros, including the ones at $0$ and $\infty$

- Write $X(z)$ as a ratio of polynomials in $z$ (multiply through by a power of $z$) to see every pole and zero.
- A rational function has **as many poles as zeros** once you count those at $z=0$ and $z=\infty$. If the numerator degree in $z$ is lower than the denominator degree by $k$, there are $k$ zeros at $\infty$; if higher by $k$, there are $k$ poles at $\infty$.
- **Multiplying by $z^{-k}$** (delay by $k$) adds $k$ poles at the origin, or cancels zeros there.
- For real sequences, complex poles and zeros come in conjugate pairs.

### 5.6 What time reversal does to a pole-zero plot

$x[-n]\leftrightarrow X(1/z)$ sends every pole and zero through $z\to1/z$:

| Location in $X(z)$ | Location in $X(1/z)$ |
|---|---|
| $c=\rho e^{j\theta}$ | $\dfrac1c=\dfrac1\rho e^{-j\theta}$ |
| $0$ | $\infty$ |
| $\infty$ | $0$ |
| ROC $r_1 < \lvert z\rvert < r_2$ | $\dfrac{1}{r_2} < \lvert z\rvert < \dfrac{1}{r_1}$ |

Inside the unit circle swaps with outside, so a causal sequence becomes a left-sided one.

**Flip plus shift:** $x[-n+n_0]=x[-(n-n_0)]$ is the flipped sequence delayed by $n_0$:

$$
x[-n+n_0] \longleftrightarrow z^{-n_0}X(1/z)
$$

Do not forget the zeros of $X(z)$ at $\infty$. They move to the origin and cancel some of the poles that $z^{-n_0}$ adds there. (This is the mistake in the posted solution to 3.37: it reports a triple pole at $z=0$, but two of the three are cancelled and only a simple pole remains.)

Time-domain cross-check: if $X(z)\sim z^{-m}$ as $z\to\infty$, then $x[n]$ starts at $n=m$.

### 5.7 Inverse z-transform

**Step 0: get the ROC.** It is given, or implied by "causal", "stable", "left-sided", or by intersecting ROCs in a product.

**Partial fractions.** Write $X(z)=\dfrac{B(z)}{A(z)}$ in powers of $z^{-1}$, with numerator order $M$ and denominator order $N$.

1. If $M\ge N$, **divide first**. The quotient is a polynomial in $z^{-1}$ and inverts to impulses:

   $$X(z)=\sum_{r=0}^{M-N}B_r z^{-r}+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{A_k}{1-d_kz^{-1}}$$

   When $M=N$ the single constant is the ratio of the highest-order coefficients, $B_0=b_M/a_N$.

2. Residue at each simple pole:

   $$A_k=\left(1-d_kz^{-1}\right)X(z)\Big\rvert_{z=d_k}$$

3. Invert **term by term, using the ROC for each pole**:

   | Pole $d_k$ relative to the ROC | $\dfrac{A_k}{1-d_kz^{-1}}$ inverts to |
   |---|---|
   | ROC is outside $\lvert d_k\rvert$ | $A_k d_k^{n} u[n]$ |
   | ROC is inside $\lvert d_k\rvert$ | $-A_k d_k^{n} u[-n-1]$ |

*"inside" means closer to the origin than the ring

A repeated pole gives $\dfrac{C}{(1-dz^{-1})^2}\leftrightarrow C (n+1)d^n u[n]$ for the right-sided case.

**Other methods.** A finite polynomial in $z^{\pm1}$ inverts by inspection, coefficient by coefficient. A shifted standard form inverts with the time-shift property: a factor $z$ in front advances the sequence by one sample.

**Rewriting trick.** Force a sequence into a tabulated shape before transforming: $\left(\tfrac12\right)^{n-1}u[n+1]=4\left(\tfrac12\right)^{n+1}u[n+1]$, which is $4a^m u[m]$ advanced by one sample.

## 6. System function $H(z)$

### 6.1 Definition and the difference equation

$$
H(z)=\frac{Y(z)}{X(z)}=\sum_n h[n]z^{-n}
$$

A linear constant-coefficient difference equation and a rational $H(z)$ are the same information. Coefficients transfer directly:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{N}a_k y[n-k]=\sum_{k=0}^{M}b_k x[n-k]\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad H(z)=\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{M}b_kz^{-k}}{\sum_{k=0}^{N}a_kz^{-k}}
$$

To go from factored $H(z)$ to the difference equation: **expand the denominator**, cross-multiply, and read $z^{-k}$ as a delay of $k$.

The difference equation alone does not fix the ROC. You need an extra statement (causal, stable) to pick one.

### 6.2 Causality and stability from the ROC

| Property | Time domain | z-domain |
|---|---|---|
| Causal | $h[n]=0$ for $n < 0$ | ROC is outside the outermost pole **and includes $\infty$** |
| Stable | $\sum\lvert h[n]\rvert < \infty$ | ROC **includes the unit circle** |
| Causal and stable | both | **all poles strictly inside the unit circle** |

- Causal and stable are independent. A system can be stable but noncausal, or causal but unstable.
- A pole at $\infty$ (numerator degree in $z$ larger than denominator degree) means $h[n]$ has samples at negative $n$: **not causal**. A factor of $z$ in $H(z)$ is an advance.
- Zeros do not affect stability or causality.

### 6.3 Finding $H(z)$ from one input/output pair

$$
H(z)=\frac{Y(z)}{X(z)}
$$

The ROC of $H$ must be chosen so that its intersection with the ROC of $X$ gives the ROC of $Y$. Then test causality and stability on that ROC.

### 6.4 Output for a given input

$$
Y(z)=H(z)X(z),\qquad\text{ROC}_Y\supseteq\text{ROC}_H\cap\text{ROC}_X
$$

- A causal system driven by a **left-sided input** gives a ring-shaped ROC and a **two-sided output**: the system's poles produce right-sided terms and the input's pole produces a left-sided term.
- **Pole-zero cancellation:** a zero of $H$ at an input pole removes that mode from the output. A zero at $z=1$ cancels the pole of a step, so the step response decays to zero.

### 6.5 Inverse system

$$
H_i(z)=\frac{1}{H(z)},\qquad X(z)=\frac{Y(z)}{H(z)}
$$

Poles and zeros swap. To find the input that produces a desired output, divide.

### 6.6 Frequency response from $H(z)$

If the ROC includes the unit circle:

$$
H(e^{j\omega})=H(z)\Big\rvert_{z=e^{j\omega}}
$$

Values to substitute quickly:

| $\omega$ | $z=e^{j\omega}$ | $z^{-1}$ | $z^{-2}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $0$ | $1$ | $1$ | $1$ |
| $\pi/2$ | $j$ | $-j$ | $-1$ |
| $\pi$ | $-1$ | $-1$ | $1$ |

- **DC gain** $H(1)=\sum_n h[n]$: the response to a constant input, and the final value of the step response of a stable causal system.
- $H(-1)$: the gain for $(-1)^n$.
- General eigenfunction: $z_0^n$ for all $n$ gives $H(z_0) z_0^n$, provided $z_0$ is in the ROC.

**Geometric view.** With zeros $c_k$ and poles $d_k$:

$$
\lvert H(e^{j\omega})\rvert=\lvert b_0\rvert \frac{\prod_k\lvert e^{j\omega}-c_k\rvert}{\prod_k\lvert e^{j\omega}-d_k\rvert}
$$

The product of distances to the zeros over the product of distances to the poles. A pole near the unit circle at angle $\omega_0$ makes a peak there; a zero **on** the circle at angle $\omega_0$ nulls that frequency exactly.

## 7. Sanity checks and traps

### 7.1 Checks that take seconds

| Check | Catches errors in |
|---|---|
| Support of $x \ast h$: start and end indices add | Convolution |
| $\sum y=\sum x\cdot\sum h$ | Convolution |
| $h[0]=\lim_{z\to\infty}H(z)$ for a causal system | Partial fractions |
| $\sum_n h[n]=H(1)$ | Impulse response |
| Constant input $c$ gives output $c H(1)$ | Output for step-like inputs |
| Number of poles equals number of zeros, counting $0$ and $\infty$ | Pole-zero plots |
| Even/odd symmetry of magnitude and phase | Sketches, and whether the cosine shortcut applies |

### 7.2 Traps

1. **Not reducing the frequency.** $H(e^{j\omega})$ is given on $(-\pi,\pi]$. Reduce $\omega_0$ first.
2. **Dropping the phase sign flip** when the reduced frequency is negative.
3. **Using the cosine shortcut when $h[n]$ is not real.** Check conjugate symmetry.
4. **Multiplying by $\delta$ versus convolving with $\delta$.** One samples, the other shifts.
5. **Downsampling.** $x[2n]$ throws the odd samples away.
6. **$x[n_0-n]$.** Flip, then shift right by $n_0$.
7. **Skipping long division** when numerator order is at least denominator order. You lose the $\delta$ terms.
8. **Left-sided inversion.** Both the minus sign and $u[-n-1]$ are required.
9. **Saying a pole is "in" the ROC.** It never is. The ROC is outside or inside a pole's radius.
10. **Forgetting to intersect ROCs** for $Y=HX$.
11. **Evaluating $H(e^{j\omega})$ for an unstable system**, or using the steady-state formula for an input that starts at $n=0$ without mentioning the transient.
12. **Ignoring poles and zeros at $0$ and $\infty$** when applying $z\to1/z$ or multiplying by $z^{\pm k}$.
13. **The $0/0$ sample of a sinc.** $\dfrac{\sin(\omega_c n)}{\pi n}\to\dfrac{\omega_c}{\pi}$ at $n=0$.
14. **Causal does not imply stable**, and stable does not imply causal.
